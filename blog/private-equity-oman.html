What PE actually looks like for an Oman operator

Not a US-style LBO. Family control versus capital, governance that has to work in Muscat, and the one sentence you should be able to say before anyone drafts.

What people mean by private equity in Oman is rarely what a US leveraged buyout looks like. Control is not assumed. Families did not build a company for thirty years to hand the steering wheel to a fund because a model said 3x. Capital that works here usually sits beside the operator: growth, a recapitalisation, a minority with real governance, or a partnership where the family keeps the name and the customer relationships and the investor gets information rights that actually mean something.

I have worked PE operations on funds over $1B, and I have sat on the operator side of M&A — three transactions totaling $15M+ enterprise value. Those are different rooms. In Muscat the useful conversation is the second one dressed in the language of the first: what is the family actually selling, what are they keeping, and can the capital live with that. For GCC operators and investors who are serious about this market.

Control versus capital

The first question is not valuation. It is control. Does the family want a partner or an exit. Many say partner and mean a cheque with no change to cousins on the payroll. Many investors say partnership and mean a US-style board pack, a professional CEO in year one, and a put. Both can be honest. They are not the same deal.

In GCC family groups, the operating company is often one node in a wider set of relationships — land, other trading entities, personal guarantees at the bank. A clean PE structure that ignores those links will stall, or close and then fight. I do not pretend to be legal counsel on guarantees, ownership rules, or Sharia-compliant structures. You need proper advisers for that. I do insist that someone maps who actually decides, who is on the facilities, and what “we” means when the father and the sons are both in the room.

If you need 51% to sleep at night, say so early. If the family will not give it, you are doing minority or you are walking. Do not spend six months on a CIM to discover that. Price comes after that sentence, not before.

Governance that works in this market

Governance here is not a template. A board that meets and does not decide is decoration. What works, in my experience: a small board, information rights that include cash and aging — not only P&L — reserved matters that are few and real (related-party, new debt, capex above a line, hiring of the GM), and a cadence the family will actually keep.

PE ops work I have done on large funds was about making portfolio companies produce numbers you could run. The same muscle applies at SME scale in Oman. If DSO, inventory, and weekly cash are not on the table, you do not have governance. You have a shareholder agreement. Family businesses will protect reputation and employees. That is not irrational. An investor who treats that as noise will lose the room. An operator-partner who can show how ~45% margin improvement in a turnaround, or a 78-to-45 day DSO move unlocking $500K+ of working capital, came from rhythm rather than humiliation, will keep it. The work is still hard. It does not have to be theatrical.

What partnership actually looks like

Five early-stage investments in fintech and agritech in MENA taught me the other end of the spectrum: small cheques, local context, and no illusion that a term sheet is a company. For a manufacturing or logistics SME in Oman, the useful PE conversation is closer to this: capital for a plant or a regional push, a partner who has run a P&L, and a three-year plan that the next generation can live with.

The M&A I have closed was not a process auction. It was alignment on who runs what after signing, a working-capital true-up that did not become a war, and local partners who could actually execute. If you are an international fund looking at Oman, the local partner is not a box on a checklist. They are the reason the deal either clears or sits with a family and a set of facilities for a year. Sectors I know — logistics, manufacturing, technology — all have the same tell: if you cannot describe the week after close in operating language, you are buying a story.

Operators on the other side should ask the same of capital. Have they sat in a plant. Have they collected. Have they lived with a family board. Capital that has only seen control deals will fight you on the wrong things.

Takeaway

If you cannot say in one sentence whether the family is selling control or raising capital, you are not ready to draft. Write that sentence. Then talk about price. Governance is cash, aging, and a few reserved matters the room will honour — not a pack that nobody reads.
